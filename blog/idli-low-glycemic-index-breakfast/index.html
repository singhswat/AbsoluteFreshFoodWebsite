Idli Has One of the Lowest Glycemic Index Scores of Any Breakfast Food

By Absolute Fresh Food  ·  September 6, 2026  ·  6 min read
← Back to Blog

A slice of white toast will spike your blood sugar almost as fast as a spoonful of sugar. A bowl of cornflakes isn't much better. But a plate of soft, steaming idli — made the traditional way — barely moves the needle. If that sounds surprising for a dish made almost entirely of rice, it should. And the reason comes down to one word: fermentation.

Glycemic index (GI) measures how quickly a food raises your blood sugar after eating it. High-GI foods cause a sharp spike, followed by an energy crash an hour or two later — the mid-morning slump so many of us blame on "just being tired." Low-GI foods release glucose slowly and steadily, keeping you fuller and more even for longer. And idli, against all odds for a rice-based dish, sits firmly in the low-to-moderate GI range.

Wait — Rice Is Supposed to Be High GI

You're right to be skeptical. Plain white rice has a GI of around 70–90, depending on the variety — solidly in the "high" category. So how does a dish that's roughly 70% rice end up with a GI closer to 40–50, on par with foods like oats or legumes?

The answer is what happens between the grinding and the steaming: fermentation. When rice and urad dal soak, get ground, and ferment for 8–16 hours, naturally occurring bacteria don't just create texture and flavor — they actively restructure the starches. Fermentation partially breaks down the rapidly-digestible starch molecules and increases the proportion of what's called "resistant starch," a type your body digests much more slowly. Less rapidly available starch means less of a blood sugar spike after you eat.

The Role of Urad Dal

The lentil half of the equation matters just as much. Urad dal is rich in protein and fiber, both of which slow gastric emptying — the rate at which food leaves your stomach and enters your small intestine, where sugars actually get absorbed. Protein and fiber essentially put a brake on how fast the rice starches hit your bloodstream. This is the same principle behind why eating rice with dal, in general, produces a gentler blood sugar response than eating rice alone.

💡 Quick comparison: White bread has a GI of roughly 75. Cornflakes sit around 80. A cup of idli batter, properly fermented, produces idlis with an estimated GI in the 40s — closer to whole oats than to toast.

Why Fermentation Time Actually Changes the Number

This is the part most people don't know: the GI of your idli isn't fixed. It depends on how well — and how long — your batter actually ferments. Under-fermented batter (rushed, or made with dead/weak culture) retains more of the rapidly-digestible starch structure from raw rice. Properly fermented batter, left to develop for the full window, has undergone more of the starch transformation that lowers its glycemic impact.

In other words, the same recipe can produce a meaningfully different blood sugar response depending entirely on fermentation quality — which is one more reason live, active batter matters far more than most people realize.

What This Means If You're Watching Your Blood Sugar

For anyone managing blood sugar — whether due to prediabetes, diabetes, or just wanting to avoid the 11 AM energy crash — breakfast choice matters enormously, since it sets the tone for the rest of the day's glucose response. A low-GI breakfast like idli, especially paired with a protein-rich chutney, tends to produce steadier energy and less mid-morning hunger than a high-GI breakfast of toast, cereal, or sugary tea and biscuits.

That said, portion and pairing still matter. Two or three idlis with chutney is a very different metabolic event than six idlis with a sugary filter coffee. Balance still counts — but the base dish itself has real science-backed advantages that most people never learn about.

How to Get the Full Benefit

  • Use batter that's properly fermented for its full window (8–16 hours at room temperature) — not rushed or artificially preserved
  • Pair idli with protein-rich chutneys like podi or peanut chutney to further slow digestion
  • Avoid packaged batter with preservatives, which can inhibit the very fermentation that lowers the GI in the first place
  • Skip the excess ghee-soaked or deep-fried variations if blood sugar is a specific concern — steamed idli is the version the science actually supports

🧪 The bigger picture: This is the same reason fermented foods worldwide — from sourdough bread to Korean kimchi rice bowls — tend to have gentler blood sugar effects than their unfermented counterparts. Fermentation isn't just a flavor technique. It's a metabolic one.

The Bottom Line

Idli's low glycemic index isn't a coincidence or a myth — it's a direct result of proper fermentation transforming rice starch into something your body handles far more gently. But that only works if the batter was genuinely, fully fermented in the first place. Preserved, factory-rushed, or under-fermented batter doesn't get you the same benefit — it just gets you rice in a different shape.
